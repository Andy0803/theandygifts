The Full Review

What the "4-in-1" Actually Means

The Highland plays vinyl records across all three speeds (33⅓, 45, and 78 RPM) via a belt-driven turntable. The other three functions are what make it a gift rather than just a turntable: Bluetooth lets him stream Spotify or Apple Music through the built-in speakers wirelessly from up to 33 feet; FM radio with an illuminated analog dial brings in a detail that owners consistently mention as a standout; and AUX-in means he can plug in a phone with a cable if needed. There's also an RCA output so he can connect it to a better speaker system later if the hobby takes hold.

In practice, this means the machine earns its shelf space even on days when he isn't playing records. It's a piece of furniture that does four things.

The Build & the Looks

The Highland comes in Espresso, Farmhouse Walnut, and Forest Green — all three colorways photograph beautifully and look intentional rather than cheap on a shelf or sideboard. The cabinet is wood-finish MDF with a fabric-grille front; the platter and tonearm are plastic. This is where the honest caveat lives: the overall build is lightweight, and the tonearm mechanism is basic. It does the job, but it won't feel like a piece of precision equipment when you handle it.

Dimensions are 20.47" × 16.34" × 9.45" — substantial enough to look serious, compact enough for a standard shelf.

The Sound

The built-in speakers produce 10W RMS (40W peak), which is enough to fill a bedroom or a home office comfortably. Owner reports describe the sound as warm and enjoyable for casual listening — "it really does fill up a room" is a phrase that recurs. The frequency response (60Hz–20kHz) covers the full audible range adequately for the genre.

The honest limit: this is not a Hi-Fi setup. Compared directly to the Audio-Technica AT-LP60X (which costs similar money but requires separate speakers), the AT-LP60X produces noticeably better audio fidelity. The Victrola's tonearm is lighter and tracks with less precision. For someone who has a critical ear and an existing speaker system, the AT-LP60X is the better buy. For someone who wants a plug-in-and-play machine that sounds genuinely good for casual listening — and looks beautiful doing it — the Highland holds its own.

The Key Trade-off: Victrola vs. Audio-Technica

This comes up in every beginner turntable conversation and it's worth naming directly. If sound quality is the priority, the Audio-Technica AT-LP60X is the better machine — better tonearm balance, better tracking, cleaner output. But it has no speakers, no Bluetooth, no FM radio, and it requires a separate amplifier to produce any sound at all. For a gift, that means handing someone a turntable they can't actually hear until they've bought another £100–200 of equipment.

The Highland's advantage is that it works on its own, day one, out of the box. For a gift — especially to someone re-entering the vinyl world after a long absence — that completeness is worth more than marginal audio fidelity.

Is It a Good Gift?

Yes — for a specific person. The man who grew up with records and still has them. The guy who keeps meaning to "get back into vinyl." The person who would love the ritual of putting on a record on a Sunday morning but would never navigate the complexity of a separates system. For all of these, the Highland is the right call: one machine, one cable, everything he needs.

It's the wrong gift for the person who already owns a turntable and a decent speaker setup (they want better not more), or for the dedicated audiophile who will immediately notice the tonearm's limitations. For anyone in between — it's one of the most complete and giftable record players at this price.
